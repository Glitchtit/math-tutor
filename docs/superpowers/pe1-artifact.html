Negative marking: when to guess

The penalty per wrong answer is not stated on the slide; check Moodle or ask. Here is the expected gain from guessing on a 4-option question, in units of one correct answer, when you guess at random among the options you could not rule out:

Options left after ruling some outChance rightPenalty −0.25Penalty −0.33Penalty −0.5Penalty −1
4 (no idea)25 %+0.060.00−0.13−0.50
3 (one ruled out)33 %+0.17+0.110.00−0.33
2 (two ruled out)50 %+0.38+0.33+0.250.00

How it is calculated: expected gain = P(right) × 1 − P(wrong) × penalty. Example, 3 options left at −0.5: \(\frac13\cdot1-\frac23\cdot0.5=0.33-0.33=0\).

Rule of thumb. Leave it blank if you truly have no idea. Guess once you can rule out at least one option, unless the penalty is −1 (then only with two ruled out, and even that only breaks even). For fill-in blanks there is no option list, so leave it blank unless you are fairly sure of the term.

Using the formula sheet well

  • Before the exam, go through the formula sheet below and say for each line when it applies and what each symbol is. Your real sheet will look different but contain the same physics.
  • In a calculation, write the formula in symbols, rearrange in symbols, then insert numbers with units. Partial credit usually follows the written steps.
  • Convert prefixes first: k = \(10^3\), m = \(10^{-3}\), µ = \(10^{-6}\), n = \(10^{-9}\).
  • Finish with a check: power in = power out for ideal converters; \(V_o<V_s\) for buck, \(V_o>V_s\) for boost; \(0\le D\le1\).

00 / START HEREHow to read the math on this page

Every derivation is a list of small steps. Each step changes one thing. Three aids tell you what changed:

1. The chip says what kind of move it is

Start
An equation we take from a law or a picture. Nothing is changed yet.
Substitute
Swap a symbol for something it equals. Like replacing a word with its definition.
Both sides
Do the same operation to the left and right side (add, subtract, multiply, divide). The equation stays true.
Expand / factor
Multiply out a bracket, or pull a common piece back into a bracket. Same value, different shape.
Cancel
Remove things that add up to zero (\(+x-x\)) or divide to one (\(\frac{x}{x}\)).
Calculus
Take a derivative (slope) or an integral (area). The toolkit below explains both with drawings.

2. Colour follows a term from line to line

If a term is coloured, look for the same colour in the next line: that is where it went or what it became. Crossed-out terms are cancelled. Example:

  1. Start
    \[V_s=\A{v_R}+v_C\qquad\text{and we know}\qquad \A{v_R}=\A{R\,i}\]
    Blue marks the piece that is about to be swapped.
  2. Substitute put \(\A{R\,i}\) where \(\A{v_R}\) was
    \[V_s=\A{R\,i}+v_C\]
    The blue piece is the replacement. Everything else is copied unchanged.

LECTURE 1Fundamentals of power electronics

Definition. Power electronics is the application of electronic circuits to convert, control and manage the flow of electrical power from one form or level to another. Power converters do the conversion. Heat losses are unavoidable but can be reduced.

A converter can change three things: the system (AC ↔ DC), the voltage level (LV ↔ HV) and the frequency (LF ↔ HF).

Sources and loads

  • DC sources: one current direction, constant voltage. PV, fuel cells, batteries.
  • AC sources: current periodically reverses. Synchronous generators, constant 50 Hz or 60 Hz.
  • DC loads: most consumer electronics (phone charger 3–6 V DC, laptop charger 12 V DC).
  • AC loads: electric motors, which require AC of variable frequency (fan, refrigerator: 220–240 V 50 Hz EU, 110–120 V 60 Hz US).

The motivating problems: run a DC load from AC, and rotate an AC motor from DC. Both are solved by power electronic converters.

Converter components

Active: transistor (controlled switch). Passive: diode, resistor, capacitor, inductor, transformer. Transistor and diode are the semiconductor devices. Note the diode is a semiconductor yet counted as passive, because it cannot be commanded on or off.

History

Driving forces: defence and military (major influence), industrial electrification and grids, semiconductor revolution (critical breakthrough), transportation electrification, renewables and efficiency, consumer electronics (growing importance).

LECTURE 2 · PART 1Laws and circuit components

Circuit components control, convert or condition energy. Circuit analysis examines voltage, current and power. Tools: Ohm, Kirchhoff, (Fourier). Purpose: modelling and performance evaluation (losses, dynamics, resonance).

Quantities

  • Current \(i=\dfrac{dq}{dt}\) [A]: how much charge passes per second (a rate, M6). 1 A = 1 C/s.
  • Voltage \(v=v_1-v_2\) [V]: potential difference between two points, how hard charges are pushed.
  • Power \(P=VI\) [W]: rate of energy transfer. 1 W = 1 J/s.

Laws

LawWho / whenStatement
OhmGeorg Simon Ohm, 1827Current in a conductor is proportional to the voltage across it: \(V=IR\)
FaradayMichael Faraday, 18311st: an EMF is induced in an \(N\)-turn coil when the linked flux changes. 2nd: its magnitude is proportional to the rate of change of flux. \(e=-N\,d\Phi/dt\)
LenzHeinrich Lenz, 1834The induced EMF opposes the change in flux. Lenz gives the direction (the minus sign)
KirchhoffGustav Robert Kirchhoff, 1845KCL: algebraic sum of currents at a junction is zero. KVL: around any closed loop, sum of EMFs = sum of voltage drops

Resistor power

  1. Start two known relations
    \[P=\A{V}\,I\qquad\qquad \A{V}=\A{IR}\]
    Definition of power, and Ohm's law. Blue marks \(V\), which we will swap out.
  2. Substitute \(\A{IR}\) in place of \(\A{V}\)
    \[P=\A{(IR)}\,I=I\cdot I\cdot R=I^2R\]
    \(I\cdot I\) is written \(I^2\). Order of multiplication does not matter.
  3. Result or swap \(I\) instead, using \(I=V/R\)
    \[P=I^2R=\frac{V^2}{R}\]
    \(P=V\cdot\frac VR=\frac{V^2}{R}\). All of this power becomes heat in the resistor.

Series and parallel resistors

  1. Start series: KVL adds the two drops
    \[V=\A{IR_1}+\B{IR_2}\]
    Same \(I\) through both resistors (picture, left).
  2. Factor out the common \(I\)
    \[V=I\,(R_1+R_2)\]
    Compare with Ohm's law \(V=I\cdot R\): the bracket plays the role of \(R\).
  3. Result
    \[R_s=R_1+R_2\]
  4. Start parallel: KCL adds the two branch currents
    \[\G{I}=\A{\frac{V}{R_1}}+\B{\frac{V}{R_2}}\]
    Same \(V\) across both (picture, right); each branch obeys Ohm's law.
  5. Factor out the common \(V\)
    \[\G{I}=V\left(\frac1{R_1}+\frac1{R_2}\right)\]
  6. Common denominator make both fractions have \(R_1R_2\) underneath
    \[\frac1{R_1}\cdot\C{\frac{R_2}{R_2}}+\frac1{R_2}\cdot\C{\frac{R_1}{R_1}}=\frac{R_2}{R_1R_2}+\frac{R_1}{R_1R_2}=\frac{R_1+R_2}{R_1R_2}\]
    Multiplying by \(\frac{R_2}{R_2}\) is multiplying by 1, so the value is unchanged. Numbers: \(\frac12+\frac13=\frac36+\frac26=\frac56\).
  7. ÷ both sides Ohm's law says \(R=V/I\)
    \[R_p=\frac{V}{\G{I}}=\frac{V}{V\cdot\frac{R_1+R_2}{R_1R_2}}=\frac{\X{V}}{\X{V}\cdot\frac{R_1+R_2}{R_1R_2}}\]
    \(V\) cancels top and bottom.
  8. Result 1 divided by a fraction = the fraction flipped
    \[R_p=\frac{1}{\;\frac{R_1+R_2}{R_1R_2}\;}=\frac{R_1R_2}{R_1+R_2}\]
    "Product over sum". Always smaller than the smaller resistor. Numbers: \(\frac{1}{5/6}=\frac65\).

Voltage divider (slide example: 24 V → 5 V?)

  1. Start series current from Ohm's law on the total
    \[\G{I}=\frac{V_{in}}{R_1+R_2}\]
    No load connected, so \(R_1\) and \(R_2\) are in series.
  2. Substitute into \(V_{out}=\G{I}R_2\)
    \[V_{out}=\G{\frac{V_{in}}{R_1+R_2}}\cdot R_2=\frac{R_2}{R_1+R_2}\,V_{in}\]
    The output is the voltage across \(R_2\). Each resistor takes a share proportional to its size.
  3. Numbers \(R_1=4.7\ \Omega,\ R_2=1.2\ \Omega\)
    \[V_{out}=\frac{1.2}{4.7+1.2}\cdot24=\frac{1.2}{5.9}\cdot24=0.2034\cdot24=4.88\ \text{V}\]
    Slide rounds to 4.9 V.
  4. Check the cost
    \[I=\frac{24}{5.9}=4.07\ \text{A},\qquad P=24\cdot4.07=97.6\ \text{W}\]
    The divider burns about 98 W as heat with nothing connected, and \(V_{out}\) sags when a load is attached. That is why power electronics uses switching converters instead.

Capacitor

Stores energy in an electric field between two plates separated by a dielectric (insulator full of dipoles that align with the field). Unit farad [F]. Blocks DC, passes AC.

  1. Start definition of capacitance
    \[\A{q}=C\,v\]
    Charge stored is proportional to voltage; \(C\) is the fixed constant of proportionality.
  2. Differentiate both sides (M1: same operation on both sides)
    \[\frac{d\A{q}}{dt}=\frac{d}{dt}(C\,v)\]
    We want current, and current is the rate of change of charge.
  3. Substitute \(\frac{d\A{q}}{dt}=\G{i}\) (definition of current)
    \[\G{i}=\frac{d}{dt}(C\,v)\]
  4. Result constant factor rule (M6): \(C\) comes out
    \[\G{i}=C\,\frac{dv}{dt}\]
    Capacitor current is proportional to how fast its voltage changes. Steady DC voltage → slope 0 → \(i=0\): capacitor blocks DC. A sudden jump in voltage would need infinite current, so capacitor voltage cannot jump.

Same law solved for \(v\) (÷ \(C\), then integrate both sides): \(v=\frac1C\int i\,dt\), the form on the L4 recap slide.

Inductor

Stores energy in a magnetic field. Unit henry [H]. Blocks AC, passes DC.

  1. Start definition of inductance
    \[\A{\varphi_B}=L\,i\]
    Flux (linkage) is proportional to current.
  2. Substitute into Faraday's law \(v=\frac{d\A{\varphi_B}}{dt}\)
    \[v=\frac{d}{dt}(\A{L\,i})\]
    The Lenz minus sign is absorbed by drawing \(v\) as a drop in the direction of \(i\): physically the voltage opposes the change in current.
  3. Result \(L\) is constant, so it comes out
    \[v=L\,\frac{di}{dt}\]
    Steady DC current → \(v=0\): inductor is a short to DC. A sudden jump in current would need infinite voltage, so inductor current cannot jump. That is why a buck converter needs a diode to carry the inductor current when the switch opens.
Mirror image. Capacitor: \(i=C\,\frac{dv}{dt}\), voltage cannot jump, blocks DC. Inductor: \(v=L\,\frac{di}{dt}\), current cannot jump, blocks AC. Swap \(v\leftrightarrow i\) and \(C\leftrightarrow L\) to go from one to the other.
BEYOND SLIDES · why "blocks AC / DC"Reactances: \(X_C=\dfrac{1}{2\pi fC}\) (infinite at \(f=0\), so DC is blocked) and \(X_L=2\pi fL\) (zero at \(f=0\), large at high \(f\)). Stored energy: \(W_C=\tfrac12CV^2\), \(W_L=\tfrac12LI^2\).

Diode

Two terminals: anode (A) and cathode (K). Conducts A → K only. Forward bias \(V_A>V_K\): conducts. Reverse bias \(V_A<V_K\): blocks. Ideal diode: zero drop when on, zero current when off (full reverse voltage across it). Practical diode: needs about 0.7 V (Si) before conducting and leaks a little in reverse. Example: half-wave rectifier, which passes only the positive half-cycles of an AC input to the load.

Transistor (MOSFET as switch)

Terminals gate (G), drain (D), source (S). Switching signal applied between gate and source. \(V_{GS}>V_{th}\) (threshold): on, current flows D → S. \(V_{GS}=0\): off. Unlike a diode, the switching command is independent of the circuit voltages, so the transistor is active. Often drawn as a generic switch.

Half-bridge (half-wave) inverter: two switches in series across a DC supply. S1 on / S2 off connects the output to one rail, S1 off / S2 on to the other, producing a square-wave AC output. Never turn both on at once: that shorts the supply.

Power rating vs switching frequency (slide 26): thyristors/GTOs cover the highest power at low frequency (HVDC, traction), IGBTs the middle (kVA to MVA, around 1–20 kHz: drives, UPS, welding), MOSFETs the highest frequency at lower power (SMPS, 100 kHz and up).

The shortcut first

Every RC or RL switching transient has the same shape. You need three numbers: where the quantity starts, where it ends, and the time constant.

\[x(t)=x_{\text{end}}+\big(x_{\text{start}}-x_{\text{end}}\big)\,e^{-t/\tau}\qquad\tau=RC\ \text{ or }\ \tau=\frac LR\]
QuantityStart (t = 0)End (t → ∞)Plug in

The start and end values come from the two component facts: at \(t=0\) a capacitor's voltage and an inductor's current keep their old values; at \(t\to\infty\) (DC steady state) a capacitor is an open circuit and an inductor is a wire. The derivations below show where the formula itself comes from.

RC charging: full derivation

Part A: build the equation

  1. Start KVL around the loop
    \[V_s=\A{v_R}+\B{v_C}\]
    Walking once round the loop, the source lifts the potential by \(V_s\); the resistor and capacitor together drop the same amount.
  2. Substitute Ohm's law \(\A{v_R}=\A{R\,\G{i}}\)
    \[V_s=\A{R}\,\G{i}+\B{v_C}\]
    Two unknowns remain: \(\G{i}\) and \(\B{v_C}\). Next swap removes \(\G{i}\).
  3. Substitute capacitor law \(\G{i}=\G{C\frac{dv_C}{dt}}\)
    \[V_s=R\,\G{C\frac{dv_C}{dt}}+\B{v_C}\]
    Now only one unknown, \(v_C(t)\), appears (together with its own slope). An equation linking a quantity to its rate of change is a differential equation.
  4. − both sides subtract \(\B{v_C}\)
    \[\begin{aligned}V_s\C{-v_C}&=RC\frac{dv_C}{dt}+v_C\C{-v_C}\\V_s-v_C&=RC\frac{dv_C}{dt}\end{aligned}\]
    Collect the slope on one side.
  5. ÷ both sides divide by \(\C{RC}\), then read right-to-left
    \[\frac{dv_C}{dt}=\frac{V_s-v_C}{\C{RC}}\]
    Meaning: the charging speed (slope) is proportional to the gap \(V_s-v_C\) still left. Big gap at the start → steep. Gap shrinking → slope shrinking. That is why the curve flattens out and never quite reaches \(V_s\).

Part B: solve it

  1. Separate × both sides by \(\C{dt}\), ÷ both sides by \(\A{(V_s-v_C)}\)
    \[\frac{dv_C}{\A{V_s-v_C}}=\frac{\C{dt}}{RC}\]
    \(dv_C\) and \(dt\) are tiny changes; treat them like ordinary numbers. Goal: everything about \(v_C\) on the left, everything about \(t\) on the right, so each side can be summed (integrated) on its own.
  2. Integrate both sides from the start to "now"
    \[\int_{0}^{\,v_C}\frac{dv}{V_s-v}=\int_0^{\,t}\frac{dt'}{RC}\]
    Add up all tiny changes from the start state (time 0, voltage 0) to the present (time \(t\), voltage \(v_C\)). Inside the integrals the running variables are renamed \(v\) and \(t'\) so they do not get confused with the end values \(v_C\) and \(t\).
  3. Substitute left side only: let \(\A{u}=V_s-v\)
    \[\A{u}=V_s-v\quad\Rightarrow\quad d\A{u}=-dv\quad\Rightarrow\quad dv=-d\A{u}\]
    If \(v\) goes up by a tiny amount, \(u\) goes down by the same amount. The limits change too: when \(v=0\), \(u=V_s\); when \(v=v_C\), \(u=V_s-v_C\).
  4. Integrate using \(\int\frac{du}{u}=\ln u\) (M9)
    \[\int_{V_s}^{V_s-v_C}\frac{-d\A{u}}{\A{u}}=-\Big[\ln \A{u}\Big]_{V_s}^{V_s-v_C}=-\Big(\ln(V_s-v_C)-\ln V_s\Big)\]
    \([\,F\,]_a^b\) means "\(F\) at the top limit minus \(F\) at the bottom limit".
  5. Tidy \(\ln a-\ln b=\ln\frac ab\)
    \[\text{left side}=-\ln\frac{V_s-v_C}{V_s}\]
  6. Integrate right side: \(\frac1{RC}\) is a constant (rectangle, M7)
    \[\int_0^{\,t}\frac{dt'}{RC}=\frac{1}{RC}\,(t-0)=\frac{t}{RC}\]
  7. × both sides by −1 after setting left = right
    \[\begin{aligned}-\ln\frac{V_s-v_C}{V_s}&=\frac{t}{RC}\\ \ln\frac{V_s-v_C}{V_s}&=-\frac{t}{RC}\end{aligned}\]
  8. Undo the ln raise \(e\) to each side: \(e^{\ln y}=y\)
    \[\frac{V_s-v_C}{V_s}=e^{-t/RC}\]
    \(\ln\) and \(e\) cancel each other, like \(\sqrt{x}\) and \(x^2\).
  9. × both sides by \(\C{V_s}\); name \(\tau=RC\)
    \[V_s-v_C=\C{V_s}\,e^{-t/\tau}\]
    \(\tau=RC\) has units of seconds (Ω·F = s).
  10. Rearrange + \(v_C\) both sides, − \(V_se^{-t/\tau}\) both sides
    \[v_C=V_s-\A{V_s}\,e^{-t/\tau}\]
  11. Result Factor out \(\A{V_s}\)
    \[\B{v_C(t)}=\A{V_s}\left(1-e^{-t/\tau}\right),\qquad\tau=RC\]
    Check: \(t=0\): \(V_s(1-1)=0\) ✓ (empty). \(t\to\infty\): \(V_s(1-0)=V_s\) ✓ (full).

Part C: the current

  1. Substitute the result into \(\G{i}=C\frac{d\B{v_C}}{dt}\)
    \[\G{i}=C\,\frac{d}{dt}\Big[\B{V_s\left(1-e^{-t/\tau}\right)}\Big]\]
  2. Constant factor \(V_s\) comes out (M6)
    \[\G{i}=C\,V_s\,\frac{d}{dt}\Big[1-e^{-t/\tau}\Big]\]
  3. Differentiate each term (M6 sum and exponential rules)
    \[\frac{d}{dt}\big[\,1\,\big]=0,\qquad\frac{d}{dt}\big[e^{-t/\tau}\big]=-\frac1\tau e^{-t/\tau}\]\[\Rightarrow\quad\frac{d}{dt}\Big[1-e^{-t/\tau}\Big]=0-\Big(-\frac1\tau e^{-t/\tau}\Big)=+\frac1\tau e^{-t/\tau}\]
    Minus times minus is plus (M3).
  4. Substitute and \(\frac1\tau=\frac1{RC}\)
    \[\G{i}=C\,V_s\cdot\frac{1}{RC}\,e^{-t/\tau}=\frac{\X{C}\,V_s}{R\,\X{C}}\,e^{-t/\tau}\]
    \(C\) is a factor top and bottom, so it cancels (M5).
  5. Result
    \[\G{i(t)}=\frac{V_s}{R}\,e^{-t/\tau}\]
    Slide formula. Starts at \(V_s/R\) (empty capacitor acts like a wire), decays to 0 (full capacitor blocks DC). Uses: 555 timers, converter supplies, low/high-pass filters.

RL charging: full derivation

  1. Start KVL
    \[V_s=\A{v_R}+\B{v_L}\]
  2. Substitute \(\A{v_R}=\A{R\,i}\) and \(\B{v_L}=\B{L\frac{di}{dt}}\)
    \[V_s=\A{R\,\G{i}}+\B{L\frac{d\G{i}}{dt}}\]
    Only one unknown: \(\G{i}(t)\).
  3. − both sides \(\C{Ri}\), then ÷ both sides \(\C{L}\)
    \[\begin{aligned}V_s-\C{Ri}&=L\frac{di}{dt}\\ \frac{di}{dt}&=\frac{V_s-Ri}{\C{L}}\end{aligned}\]
    Same meaning as RC: the current rises fast while the gap \(V_s-Ri\) is large, and slows as it closes.
  4. Separate \(i\)-things left, \(t\)-things right
    \[\frac{di}{\A{V_s-Ri}}=\frac{dt}{L}\]
  5. Integrate both sides from (0, 0) to (\(t\), \(i\))
    \[\int_0^{\,i}\frac{di'}{V_s-Ri'}=\int_0^{\,t}\frac{dt'}{L}=\frac tL\]
    Right side: rectangle rule, \(\frac1L\) is constant.
  6. Substitute \(\A{u}=V_s-Ri'\)
    \[d\A{u}=-R\,di'\;\Rightarrow\;di'=-\frac{d\A{u}}{R};\qquad\text{limits: }V_s\to V_s-Ri\]
    Now \(u\) changes \(R\) times faster than \(i'\) (and in the opposite direction), hence the factor \(-\frac1R\).
  7. Integrate left side
    \[\int_{V_s}^{V_s-Ri}-\frac1R\frac{d\A{u}}{\A{u}}=-\frac1R\Big[\ln \A{u}\Big]_{V_s}^{V_s-Ri}=-\frac1R\ln\frac{V_s-Ri}{V_s}\]
  8. × both sides by \(-R\) after setting left = right
    \[\ln\frac{V_s-Ri}{V_s}=-\frac{R}{L}\,t=-\frac{t}{\tau},\qquad\tau=\frac LR\]
    \(\frac RL\,t=\frac{t}{L/R}\): dividing by \(\frac LR\) is multiplying by \(\frac RL\).
  9. Undo ln then × both sides by \(V_s\)
    \[V_s-Ri=V_s\,e^{-t/\tau}\]
  10. Result rearrange, then ÷ both sides by \(R\)
    \[R\,i=V_s-V_se^{-t/\tau}\quad\Rightarrow\quad\G{i(t)}=\frac{V_s}{R}\left(1-e^{-t/\tau}\right)\]
    Check: \(t=0\): 0 ✓. \(t\to\infty\): \(V_s/R\) ✓.
  11. Result inductor voltage: substitute into \(\B{v_L}=L\frac{di}{dt}\)
    \[\B{v_L}=L\cdot\frac{V_s}{R}\cdot\frac1\tau e^{-t/\tau}=\X{L}\cdot\frac{V_s}{\X{R}}\cdot\frac{\X{R}}{\X{L}}\,e^{-t/\tau}=V_s\,e^{-t/\tau}\]
    Differentiate exactly as in RC Part C; \(\frac1\tau=\frac RL\), and \(L\), \(R\) cancel. RL uses: relays, motor starting-current limiting, filters.

LECTURE 3Power semiconductors

A switch can be mechanical (button), electromechanical (relay) or electrical (MOSFET). In power electronics, switches make rectifiers, active rectifiers, DC supplies and variable-frequency drives.

Silicon and doping

  • A semiconductor conducts between a conductor and an insulator. Its conductivity changes with doping, applied voltage or temperature. Examples: Si, Ge, GaAs.
  • Silicon: most used; second most abundant element after oxygen; 4 valence electrons forming covalent bonds in a diamond-like crystal.
  • Group-3 dopant (B, Ga, In, Al) → adds holes → P-type.
  • Group-5 dopant (P, As, Sb) → adds free electrons → N-type.
  • Holes (P) and free electrons (N) are the mobile charge carriers.

Memory aid: silicon has 4. Group 3 brings one too few (a hole, Positive gap); group 5 one too many (an extra electron, Negative).

Conventional current flows + → − (Franklin, positive carriers). Electron flow is − → + (J.J. Thomson, 1897). Circuit diagrams use conventional current. In semiconductors both holes (+) and electrons (−) carry current.

PN junction diode (Russell Ohl, Bell Labs, 1939; commercial ~1947)

BiasWhat happens
ZeroHoles diffuse P → N, electrons N → P. This leaves negative charge on the P side and positive on the N side: the depletion layer. Its electric field stops further diffusion and forms the potential barrier. Width depends on doping levels.

Schottky and PIN diodes

Schottky (theory: Walter Schottky 1938; commercial 1960s): P-type replaced by a metal (Al, W, Mo, Pt, Cr) forming a metal–semiconductor junction. The Schottky barrier is much smaller, because there is no P-type: only electrons are majority carriers.

PIN (Jun-ichi Nishizawa 1950; commercial ~1952): p+ (heavy), n− (lightly doped "intrinsic", wide), n+ (heavy). Zero bias: two depletion layers. Forward: carriers injected into the intrinsic layer, low-resistance switch. Reverse: depletion regions widen and merge into one large layer, high-resistance switch. The intrinsic layer acts as a voltage-controlled resistor.

How each switch works

BJT on: base–emitter junction forward biased, collector–base reverse biased. Carriers are injected from emitter into the base; the base is thin, so they cross into the collector. Off: base–emitter reverse biased, no current.

SCR modes:

  • Forward blocking (anode +): outer junctions J1, J3 forward biased, middle J2 reverse biased → blocks. No gate current, stays off.
  • Reverse blocking (anode −): J1, J3 reverse biased, J2 forward → blocks. Gate signal does not help; stays off.
  • Forward conduction: a sufficient gate–cathode current pulse injects holes, the middle depletion layer breaks down, device conducts. The outer layers then keep supplying carriers regardless of the gate: latched on. Turn-off requires reverse bias across A–K.

MOSFET: gate–oxide–body forms a capacitor. Off: non-positive \(V_{GS}\), junctions blocked by depletion layers. On: positive \(V_{GS}\) repels holes under the oxide and draws electrons in from n+ regions, creating an inversion n layer (the channel). Drain–source voltage then drives electrons through all-n path; majority carriers only, no diffusion current. Modes: enhancement = normally open (off at \(V_{GS}=0\)); depletion = normally closed.

IGBT: positive \(V_{GE}\) creates the N channel (MOSFET mechanism). That electron current drives hole injection from the outer p+ layer into the n− drift layer, which removes the inner depletion layer (BJT mechanism) and gives a hole current path too. Result: MOS-easy gate drive with bipolar-low conduction loss at high current.

MOSFET vs BJT (slide 29)

MOSFET advantages: faster switching; higher input impedance (less switching/drive loss); no built-in forward voltage drop (no potential barrier in the channel); smaller (higher power density). Wide bandgap (WBG)

  • Bandgap 2–3× that of silicon. Materials: GaN, SiC. Benefits: higher temperature and voltage tolerance, higher efficiency, more compact.
  • SiC MOSFET: same MOS structure as Si-MOSFET on a SiC substrate; the drift layer is much thinner and more heavily doped → lower on-resistance.
  • GaN: no doped regions. A heterojunction of AlGaN and GaN; because the bandgaps differ, electrons accumulate at the interface forming a 2DEG (two-dimensional electron gas), a nanometre-thin channel with very high electron mobility. Usually GaN-on-Si or GaN-on-SiC substrates for cost and thermal reasons.
  • Trend: SiC MOSFETs and GaN devices are replacing Si transistors; MOSFET and IGBT remain the most used so far.

LECTURE 4DC-DC converters

A DC-DC converter steps a DC voltage down or up. It needs a switching device; the output level is set by the duty cycle \(D\) (fraction of the period the switch is on, \(0\le D\le1\)) at switching frequency \(f_s=1/T_s\) (how fast it toggles).

Average output of a chopped voltage

A switch alone between source and load gives the pulse train in figure M8: \(v_o=V_s\) while on (\(0\) to \(DT_s\)), \(v_o=0\) while off (\(DT_s\) to \(T_s\)).

  1. Start definition of average (M8)
    \[V_o=\frac1{T_s}\int_0^{T_s}v_o(t)\,dt\]
    Area of one period, divided by the period.
  2. Split the area where the switch changes state (M7)
    \[V_o=\frac1{T_s}\Big[\A{\int_0^{DT_s}V_s\,dt}+\B{\int_{DT_s}^{T_s}0\,dt}\Big]\]
    On-piece has height \(V_s\); off-piece has height 0.
  3. Rectangle rule height × width
    \[V_o=\frac1{T_s}\Big[\A{V_s\cdot DT_s}+\B{0}\Big]\]
  4. Result Cancel \(T_s\)
    \[V_o=\frac{V_s\,D\,\X{T_s}}{\X{T_s}}=DV_s\]
    Example: 24 V at \(D=0.5\) → 12 V average.

Problem: the real output is a pulse train, \(v_o(t)=V_o+\Delta v\) (ripple). Fix: add an LC low-pass filter (smoothing). It blocks the AC components and leaves the DC average. Practical ripple remains around 1–3 %.

Steady state and small-ripple approximation

Steady state: after all transients have settled. Waveforms still ripple, but every waveform repeats exactly each period. Small-ripple approximation: ripple in capacitor voltage and inductor current is small compared with the average, so treat \(v_o\approx V_o\) and \(i_L\approx I_L\) as constants during each on/off interval. That turns every integral into a rectangle.

Why volt-second and amp-second balance hold

  1. Start inductor law, then ÷ both sides by \(L\)
    \[v_L=L\frac{di_L}{dt}\quad\Rightarrow\quad\frac{di_L}{dt}=\frac{v_L}{L}\]
  2. Integrate both sides over one period
    \[\int_0^{T_s}\frac{di_L}{dt}\,dt=\frac1L\int_0^{T_s}v_L\,dt\]
    \(\frac1L\) is a constant, so it sits outside the integral.
  3. Left side sum of all small changes = total change (M7)
    \[\A{i_L(T_s)-i_L(0)}=\frac1L\int_0^{T_s}v_L\,dt\]
    Left side: current at the end of the period minus current at the start.
  4. Volt-second balance steady state: the waveform repeats
    \[\A{i_L(T_s)=i_L(0)}\;\Rightarrow\;\A{0}=\frac1L\int_0^{T_s}v_L\,dt\;\Rightarrow\;\int_0^{T_s}v_L\,dt=0\]
    Multiply both sides by \(L\). In words: the area under \(v_L\) over one period is zero, so the average inductor voltage is zero. If it were not, the current would creep up (or down) every cycle, which is not steady state.
  5. Amp-second balance same three steps with \(i_C=C\frac{dv_C}{dt}\)
    \[v_C(T_s)-v_C(0)=\frac1C\int_0^{T_s}i_C\,dt=0\;\Rightarrow\;\int_0^{T_s}i_C\,dt=0\]
    Average capacitor current over one period is zero.

P10 · CONCEPTExplain why a buck converter needs the diode, and why its output can never exceed the input.Show solution

When S opens, the inductor current cannot change instantly (\(v=L\,di/dt\) would be infinite). The diode gives that current a freewheeling path from ground through L to the output. Without it, the voltage across the opening switch would spike and destroy it.

EXAM PRACTICE · THEORYShort theory questions

Answer in 2–4 sentences, out loud or on paper, then compare. A good exam answer names the mechanism and the consequence.

T1Why does a switch-mode converter waste far less power than a linear regulator or resistive divider?Show model answer

A resistive element drops the excess voltage while carrying the full current, so \(P=VI\) is lost as heat. An ideal switch is either fully on (current but ~0 V) or fully off (voltage but ~0 A), so \(P=VI\approx0\) in both states. Energy is moved through L and C, which store and release it without loss; only switching transitions and non-ideal resistances cause losses.

T2Explain how the depletion layer forms and what happens to it under forward and reverse bias.Show model answer

At the junction, holes diffuse from P to N and electrons from N to P. They leave behind fixed ions, which creates a charge-free depletion layer with an electric field that stops further diffusion (the potential barrier). Forward bias (+ on P) pushes carriers towards the junction; above the knee voltage the layer collapses and current flows. Reverse bias widens the layer; only a small leakage current flows until breakdown.

T3Compare a Schottky diode with a PN diode. When would you choose each?Show model answer

Schottky uses a metal–N junction with only majority carriers, so its barrier is smaller: lower forward drop, lower on-resistance, faster switching, but lower breakdown voltage and higher leakage. Choose Schottky for low-voltage, high-frequency converters where conduction and switching losses matter. Choose PN (or PIN for high voltage) where blocking voltage and low leakage matter more.

T4Describe the three operating modes of an SCR and why it cannot be turned off from the gate.Show model answer

Forward blocking: anode positive, outer junctions J1 and J3 forward biased, middle J2 reverse biased, so it blocks. Reverse blocking: anode negative, J1 and J3 reverse biased, so it blocks even with a gate signal. Forward conduction: a gate current pulse injects carriers, J2 breaks down and the device conducts. Once on, the outer layers keep supplying carriers to the middle layers by themselves (latching), so the gate loses control; it turns off only when the anode–cathode is reverse biased.

T5How does an N-channel enhancement MOSFET turn on? Why does it switch faster than a BJT?Show model answer

The gate–oxide–body forms a capacitor. A positive \(V_{GS}\) above threshold pushes holes away from under the oxide and pulls electrons in, forming an n-type inversion channel between drain and source; current then flows from D to S. Only majority carriers (electrons) conduct and the gate is insulated, so there is no stored minority charge to remove and almost no gate current: fast switching and low drive power. The cost is a higher on-resistance and lower current rating.

T6Why is the IGBT preferred for high-power motor drives and EV charging?Show model answer

It combines a MOSFET-style insulated gate (easy, low-power drive) with bipolar conduction: the channel current causes hole injection into the drift layer, which lowers the on-state resistance at high current. It handles very high voltage (> 1 kV) and current (> 500 A) with low conduction loss, at medium switching speed. MOSFETs are faster but have higher on-resistance and lower current rating.

T7What advantages do wide-bandgap devices (SiC, GaN) have, and how does GaN conduct?Show model answer

A bandgap 2–3× that of silicon allows higher voltage and temperature, higher efficiency and smaller converters. SiC MOSFETs have the same MOS structure but a thinner, more heavily doped drift layer, so lower on-resistance. GaN has no doped regions: at the AlGaN/GaN heterojunction electrons gather into a 2DEG, a nanometre-thin channel with very high electron mobility.

T8State volt-second balance and explain why it must hold in steady state.Show model answer

In steady state the integral of the inductor voltage over one switching period is zero, i.e. the average inductor voltage is zero. From \(v_L=L\,di_L/dt\), that integral equals \(L\,[i_L(T_s)-i_L(0)]\). In steady state the current waveform repeats, so the current at the end of a period equals that at the start, and the integral must be zero. Otherwise the current would grow or shrink every cycle.

T9What is the small-ripple approximation and why is it needed?Show model answer

It assumes the ripple in capacitor voltage and inductor current is small compared with their average values, so they are treated as constants (\(v_o\approx V_o\), \(i_L\approx I_L\)) within each switching interval. This makes the inductor voltage and capacitor current piecewise constant, so the balance integrals become simple height × width products that give \(V_o/V_s\) and \(I_o/I_L\).

T10Why do a capacitor and an inductor at the output of a chopper give a smooth DC output?Show model answer

The chopped voltage is a DC average plus AC components at the switching frequency and its harmonics. The LC network is a low-pass filter: the inductor opposes fast current changes (high impedance at high frequency) and the capacitor shunts fast voltage changes (low impedance at high frequency). The DC average passes through; only a small ripple (about 1–3 %) remains.
